01

Media bias

Credible isn't the same as unbiased

Media Bias Outlet · analysing Sasha Luccioni's TED Talk

My analysis of media bias in the TED Talk "AI is dangerous, but not for the reasons you think," presented by Sasha Luccioni.

Point

Bias isn't just in the words (language and emotion) a source uses, it can be in the images (visuals) and examples (voices heard) the source chooses to show.

Evidence

In the TED Talk I analysed, the AI-generated images mostly showed scientists as white men, and phrases such as "a distraction" pushed the audience toward one view.

Explanation

It showed me a source can be credible and still be biased since it only comes from one perspective (researchers, artists, victims of AI bias) rather than other possible perspectives (AI developers, AI engineers).

Jigsaw Expert Groups · Visuals & Footage

My contribution to the Jigsaw Expert Groups activity, learning through one of four ways to identify media bias: Visuals & Footage.

The three photos analysed in the full presentation — shown together below.

Point

Media bias can be identified through the visuals and footage shown by a source.

Evidence

Comparing all images, each one has a different emotion (stronger emotional response, peaceful, chaotic) which can be identified by the facial expressions of the people (image 2), how the crowd stands (image 3), and how disorganized the crowd looks (image 1). We can identify what information is missing from each image, where image 1 doesn't show the protester's emotions (backshot photo), image 2 doesn't show whether the whole protest was emotional or just one person (focused on one person photo), image 3 doesn't show the issue being discussed (shot of people lining up neatly).

Explanation

It showed me how visuals and footages can help us identify media bias since a source can choose specific photos or videos to be uploaded as there are different type of shots that could be taken (from this source, it could be a backshot, focused on one person, or choosing a specific moment photo)

Project-Based Learning

Seen: a question mark disguised as an answer

PBL Final Assessment · reading "Seen" by David Cowan, CNA

My final project-based learning assessment, including the written script and the presentation delivered in class.

Teacher feedback — video coming soon
Point

The CNA article titled "How do you feel when your text message is received but not replied instantly?" by David Cowan is biased towards the negative sides of the technological invention of read receipts.

Evidence

The article emphasizes on negative words such as "anxiety, discomfort, nightmare" which make read receipts seem more stressful (use of language and emotion). The article focuses on how read receipts can "create pressure and cause anxiety," ignoring the all the benefits. Although the source is also credible as David Cowan is a psychology lecturer at the University of Queensland, a source can provide accurate information while still choosing certain language, examples, or perspectives over others.

Explanation

The article is credible and informative, so it isn't necessarily misleading because its bias comes from emphasis. It gives more attention to the negative effects, which encourages readers to see read receipts mainly as something stressful.

Final Reflection

Before

I did not fully understand how easily media can influence the way we see information, and I did not know how to identify bias in different types of sources.

During

I learned how to identify media bias through language, emotions, visuals, and different voices by reading articles and finding evidence of bias. I had some difficulty at first, especially explaining how my observations counted as evidence, but I gradually became more confident analysing sources.

After

I feel that I've improved at identifying and explaining bias. The best part of this term was presenting my analysis through Canva slides, because I felt I did really well with both my speaking and my creative presentation. My biggest learning achievement is understanding that I shouldn't automatically trust everything I see in the media — moving forward, I'll keep using these skills on news articles and social media alike.
